An indirect prompt injection that lands in an agent's long-term memory or task schedule does not have to work right away. It can sit there and execute in a later session, after the attacker has left and with no user watching. We describe a small runtime check aimed at that moment. Every stored entry carries a derivation record written by the store, not by the agent: its source channel, author, parent links and a hash chained over its parents. Before a recalled entry or a scheduled task is allowed to act, the gate recomputes the chain, re-derives the entry's authority class, and applies a fixed decision table. The gate is about 500 lines of standard-library Python, deterministic, and makes no model calls. We preregistered four hypotheses and froze the gate before testing it. On a synthetic carryover harness with a 4B open-weight agent, scheduled-task attacks fell from 15/25 to 0/25, with no false positives in 605 checks of legitimate entries. On all 90 cases of the Task-Tampering benchmark, the gate alone lowered attack success from 18.9% to 11.1%. Stacked on four of the benchmark's own defenses it lowered attack success under every one of them, by 4.4 to 12.2 points, with the largest drop on the weakest defense, as preregistered. Each condition ran once, most per-defense differences are within noise, and the gate costs task completion under the stricter defenses. It also cannot stop injections that read as plain facts. Code, both preregistrations and per-sample results are public.
